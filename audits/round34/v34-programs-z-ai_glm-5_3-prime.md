### F1 — CRITICAL: a stranger can claim a vault's deposit and reserve addresses; a claimed address steals deposits and permanently bricks the vault

**Construct.** The vault's value addresses are the six derived token accounts of its asset-signer PDA — the classic ATA derivations for the three reserve mints (these are the deposit addresses the holder guide tells holders to send to) and the Token-2022 derivations that `burn_vault` requires declared (`programs/cbob-vault-burn/src/lib.rs`, the reserve loop). **Nothing in the tree ever creates them**: `mint_vault` and `claim_charter_vault` create only the Core asset and the record; the redemption page's prior transaction (item 50's contract) creates them at *redemption* time, far too late; the deposit-address page (item 50, round 25 D9) is specified as "shows each vault's deposit address" with no creation step at all.

**The attack.** A derived address is just an address. System `create_account` requires no signature from the created account, so anyone can create a 165-byte, token-program-owned account at any of the six derivations, at any time after the mint makes the asset address public, for the rent (~1,488,440 lamports on mainnet, the SPEC's own measured figure). Then `InitializeAccount` — which requires no signature, as the bundled `tests/fixtures/mainnet-v1.json` fixture itself demonstrates (the ATA program invokes `initializeAccount3` with only the outer payer signing) — writes the attacker as the account's wallet owner. Two variants:

- **Initialized, attacker-owned (irreparable).** Every cbBTC, $GARY or $GIRAFFISH a holder sends to the vault's deposit address is a plain SPL transfer into a valid token account owned by the attacker — **the deposit is stolen**, no program of ours ever sees it. The vault can then never activate (`read_token`'s owner check → `WrongVaultAccount`), never be swept (the helper's `src_owner != signer` refusal), never be burned (the reserve loop's owner-field check → `ReserveNotOurs`) — so its contents are sealed forever. The account cannot be closed (CloseAccount needs the attacker's signature) or re-initialized. **There is no repair.**
- **Uninitialized, all-zero (repairable but undocumented).** Deposits to it fail (uninitialized), but activation, sweep and burn all refuse the same way. The repair — a permissionless `InitializeAccount(account, mint, owner = vault PDA)` — exists and makes the burn pass, but nothing in the tree knows it: the client contract, `judgeRedemptionCoverage` (which reads accounts *owned by* the PDA and so cannot see a stranger-owned account at the derived address), and the alarm's `--reconcile` (same blindness) are all silent on it.

**The Token-2022 derivations make the bricking universal.** Those addresses are *never* legitimately claimed — the ATA program refuses to create them (F4.2), and no client step creates them — so the window is not "mint to first deposit" but "mint to redemption, forever". A stranger can brick **any** vault, funded or not, activated or not, at any time before its redemption, for ~0.0015 SOL, irreparably (the initialized variant). Bricking all 2,100 costs about 3 SOL.

**Why this survived 33 rounds.** Round 33 A3 (citing round 26 L13 and round 32 A8) states the T22 derivation is "always system-owned, skipped". That premise is true only of an address nobody touched. It is false against an adversary who *deliberately* creates a token-program-owned account there — which requires no cooperation from the ATA program. R31.W2b tested only the system-owned, lamports-only variant (which the ATA create reclaims — measured ALLOW); the token-program-owned variant is untested and unhandled anywhere. The prompt's R1s(e) framing ("a Token-2022 ATA for a classic mint") presumes the ATA program is the only way to get an account at that address; it is not.

**Evidence that would exist if I am right** (one validator window on the suite's own setup, minutes): (1) System `create_account` + `InitializeAccount` at `ATA(P, cbBTC)` with owner=stranger succeeds; (2) a cbBTC transfer to that address lands in the stranger's balance; (3) `activate` refuses `WrongVaultAccount`; (4) a full redemption refuses — at the helper's `CBOB_SWEEP refused: source … is not the vault's` if the pair is named, and at BURN's `ReserveNotOurs` if it is not; (5) no instruction closes or re-initializes the stranger's account; (6) the uninitialized variant, after one permissionless `InitializeAccount` with owner=P, lets the redemption land; (7) control: a mint transaction carrying the three idempotent ATA creates leaves nothing to claim.

**Fix.** Three parts, and the first two are only possible before the lock:
1. **Program-side (owner decision, pre-lock only):** remove the Token-2022 derivations from the emptiness proof (or skip accounts there whose owner field is not the PDA). They can never hold the vault's value — Token-2022 refuses to initialize an account for a classic mint, measured F4.2 — so the check protects nothing and is a permanent, unrepairable grief surface as written. Optionally, have `mint_vault`/`claim_charter_vault` CPI the three classic ATA creations so the addresses are claimed atomically with the mint.
2. **Client contract (must be written down now):** the mint and charter-claim clients create the vault's three reserve ATAs **in the same transaction as the mint** (three `createAssociatedTokenAccountIdempotent` instructions, ~0.0045 SOL rent, packet cost to be measured — the padded-mint measurements show headroom). This closes the classic-derivation theft and the T22 window only if combined with (1); without (1), the T22 addresses must also be claimed at mint (System create + T22 initialize with owner=P), which is an awkward client step and a further argument for (1).
3. **Suite arm** pinning both variants, the repair, and the control, and a redemption-page obligation to detect and repair the uninitialized variant (the initialized variant has no repair — prevention is the only control).

Item 50's contract as currently written would not close this: it enumerates the redeem page's duties and says the deposit page "shows" the address; it never says who creates the address or checks who owns it.

## R2 / R2b. The community pool and the boost

I re-derived the invariant **"the pool always holds at least ceil(owed)"** from the code rather than the tests: `credit` adds `delta = floor(avail·SCALE/w)` to `acc` and `delta·w ≤ avail·SCALE` to `owed`, so the ceil never exceeds what was available; `claim` subtracts exact multiples of SCALE from `owed` matching whole sats paid, so `balance − paid ≥ ceil(owed_old) − paid = ceil(owed_new)`; `retire` only removes owed; `reweigh` moves owed into carry without changing it; `monthly_inline` uses `saturating_sub` and credits nothing when short. The invariant holds under every ordering I could construct.

The counters are consistent under every ordering: `pending_this_epoch ≤ pending_count` always (requests increment both; same-epoch retires decrement both; earlier-epoch retires decrement only the count; `advance_epoch` zeroes the epoch counter); `waiting_to_join` is exactly the earlier-epoch requests; F21's both-branches retire is tested. Liveness: nothing can block distributions — joins and boost expiries are permissionless, `expire_boosts` needs records only (so a vault whose token account is gone cannot stall the `BoostsExpireFirst` gate), and the `JoinWaitingFirst` gate is passable by anyone.

The boost ring is sound: two *live* (not-yet-due) boosts' end epochs always differ by less than 64 (each end ≤ purchase epoch + 63 < the other's end), so they never share a slot; a due-but-unexpired boost's slot was already consumed by `advance_epoch`, so a new boost ending 64 later lands in a clean slot. A boost earns exactly N distributions (the Nth credit runs while `dist_epoch < end`, then `advance_epoch` makes it due — BO.21/BO.35 pin both transaction orderings); nothing earned is lost or gained on any weight change (`reweigh` preserves `owed_of` exactly); the host tests drive 260 epochs and four ring wraps against independent models, and the suite's `ledger()`/`books()` recompute every total from the records after each money operation. I found no defect here.

SPEC's weaker wording on pre-join revenue is the true one and I could not break it: revenue already in the pool when a vault joins is shared at the next distribution by everyone joined then (correct — the vault enters at the standing accumulator), and limit 55's unsplit-revenue edge is as stated.

## Other findings

### F2 — MEDIUM: the shared reader's contract with the RPC is unpinned for transactions with no inner instructions

**Construct.** `bank/txread.mjs`, `validateParsedTransaction`: `need(Array.isArray(meta.innerInstructions), 'innerInstructions')`. The RPC's `getTransaction` documents `innerInstructions` as `array | null`, and transactions that perform no CPI — which on Solana is the common case for simple transfers — return `null` (or omit the field). If so, the reader throws "unreadable parsed transaction: innerInstructions" on every such transaction.

**Gain.** A large class of CBOB's own event-carrying transactions perform no CPI: direct `reschedule_reveal`, `capture_entropy`, `activate` (first call), `claim` batches that pay or join nothing, `emergency_stop_sales`/`emergency_stop_sweep`, a free `distribute(false)` that credits, `expire_boosts`, `retire_burned`. Each would be reported `tx_unreadable` (the round-32 repair keeps the pass alive) and the cursor advances past it — so the event detectors go blind for exactly the transactions that carry their events (reschedule paging, brake transitions, activation requests, distribution lines, frozen-claim deferrals), replaced by a page per transaction. Not silent, but the detection layer — which is the whole off-chain story — degrades precisely where its controls are named.

**Evidence, and the honest caveat.** One RPC call settles the premise: `getTransaction` of any plain System transfer and inspect `meta.innerInstructions`. On the suite's validator: send a bare `reschedule_reveal` and read it through `readParsedTransaction` — it either throws or parses. I could not run this, and the round-29 record cuts both ways: its log-truncation measurement used a padded stranger transaction that, if it had no inner instructions and was read successfully, would mean the RPC returns `[]` and this finding is void — but the record does not say what the padding was, and no fixture in the tree pins the no-inner shape either way (the bundled `mainnet-v1.json` has inner instructions). The reader is the shared foundation of every scanner; its RPC contract for a shape CBOB produces constantly should not be unpinned.

**Fix.** Accept `null`/absent as empty (`const inner = meta.innerInstructions ?? []`, then validate), add a selftest fixture for both the null and absent shapes, and add a live probe arm (one no-inner mainnet transaction through the real reader) to `probe-mainnet-facts.mjs`. Off-chain; resets the freeze counter; must land before the rehearsal, where it would otherwise surface as a page flood.

## GIVEN THAT NO HUMAN FIRM WILL EVER SEE THIS AND THE PROGRAMS WILL BE IMMUTABLE, what should this project do before launch that AI audit structurally cannot?

1. **Pay one Solana security engineer to attack the client surfaces as specifications, before they are built.** F1 is a known Solana attack class — "who can create the account at this address?" is a reflex for practitioners — and 34 AI rounds plus three red-teams missed it because the deposit flow lives in an unbuilt page whose entire contract is one sentence, and every prompt frames the targets around the programs. Ask a human to walk the first cbBTC deposit and the first redemption end to end, naming who creates every account and who can front-run each creation. That review costs a fraction of one round and would have found F1 in minutes.

2. **Make the dress rehearsal adversarial, not just honest.** Item 22's named pass/fail items are all correct-operation checks. Add red items with a budget: hand someone 1 SOL at the throwaway ids and a mandate to steal or brick anything they can. The planted-account attack, the log-truncation floods, the scan floods and the deploy dust are all cheap to try live and are exactly the class that reading cannot price.

3. **Exhaustively model-check the pool and the boost ring.** The arithmetic is small (u128 fixed point, one ring of 64, a handful of counters). The host tests simulate 260 random epochs; a model checker (TLA+/Apalache, or brute-force enumeration over small epoch counts and vault counts) can prove the invariants — pool ≥ ceil(owed), ring/due/active agreement, exactly-N distributions, counter consistency under every retire/join/expire interleaving — for *all* interleavings, not sampled ones. This is the one place where mechanical proof is strictly stronger than any number of AI re-derivations, and it is cheap.

4. **Generate the raw-offset readers from the source of truth.** R7-style hand recomputation is this repository's most repeated defect class, and every round re-does it by hand. Emit the Anchor IDL at build time and mechanically diff the pinned offsets, discriminators and spaces in MINT, BURN, GOV, `bank/layout.mjs` and the suite against it, as a floored gate. Generation removes the class; auditing removes one instance.

5. **Pin the RPC contract the readers depend on, live.** F2 exists because 34 rounds read the reader and no round read the RPC's answer for the shapes CBOB itself emits. A nightly probe that pushes one transaction of each shape (no-inner, v0, truncated, v1) through the real reader and asserts it parses closes that gap permanently.

6. **Run the failure runbooks with a second operator.** The hand recovery of an open revenue cycle, the deploy-dust recovery, and the guardian stop should each be executed once by someone who did not write the runbook. AI audits can read runbooks; they cannot execute them, and item 22's forced-failure walk-through is the one place a human hand is already mandated — make it a stranger's hand.

7. **Stand up a bug bounty, live from the rehearsal, sized against the 85% pool and the deposit surface, with the immutability caveat stated in its own words.** After the upgrade authority is gone, the only control that keeps working is someone paid to look. Nothing in this tree mentions a bounty anywhere, and that is the single largest gap between "audited by AI or not at all" and a system that holds real Bitcoin forever.

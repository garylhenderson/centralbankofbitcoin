## FINDING 1 — HIGH. The launch's deterministic addresses are squattable, permanently, for about 0.005 SOL, and the window is open now

**Construct.** The five program ids were claimed on mainnet 2026-10-07 with the placeholder, closing round 34's measured grief (a pre-funded ProgramData address blocks every loader deploy at that id "for good"). But the placeholder creates nothing at the addresses the launch must *create*:

- the four config PDAs — `Pubkey.findProgramAddress([CONFIG_SEED], prog)` for SEAL/MINT/BURN/GOV — created only by each `initialize`'s Anchor `init`;
- the pool ATA (`ata(cbBTC, MINT_CFG)`) and the revenue ATA, created by road step 10 and the first paid mint;
- the swap operator's wSOL and cbBTC ATAs, created by `bank/revenue.mjs`'s prepare step and by `swapInstructions` instructions 1 and 4.

All are empty today. `SystemProgram.createAccount` targets an arbitrary `to_pubkey` that only the payer signs for; anyone can create a data account at any of these addresses for the cost of rent. Nothing can ever close a squatter's account (the config PDAs cannot sign; the ATAs are fixed by derivation), so the block is permanent at the claimed ids.

**What an attacker gains.** For roughly 0.005 SOL total: a data account at any config PDA makes `initialize` fail for ever — recovery is new program ids, which is a program change that resets the freeze and re-runs the audit gate. A data account at the pool or revenue ATA blocks road step 10 and every `split_revenue` for ever (item 52's loud failure, but permanent). A data account at the operator's ATAs blocks every revenue cycle at its prepare or swap step for that key — recoverable only by a voted operator replacement (half of all live vaults plus 7 days) plus permanent hand operation of the hop. The config-PDA and ATA cases have no recovery at those addresses at all.

**Evidence that would exist if I am right.** A single `createAccount` at `MINT_CFG` (or the pool ATA) succeeds today from any wallet; afterwards `initialize` (or road step 10's idempotent create) fails with the system program's account-in-use error and no instruction can clear it. The project measured exactly this class for ProgramData in round 34 and acted on it by claiming the ids; the config-PDA and ATA variant appears nowhere in SPEC, the accepted limits, or the road steps — the V41-1 work tested the *lamports* case and treats a data account as a "conflict" (the round-41 controls assert the conflict) without stating that the conflict is permanent and the window open-ended, waiting on Gary's charter date.

**Fix.** Create every claimable address now: the pool ATA, the revenue ATA and the operator's two ATAs can all be created before `initialize` (an ATA's wallet need not exist or sign; road step 10's idempotent creates then become no-ops). Run road step 10 promptly once the charter date is set rather than leaving the ids claimed-but-uninitialized for weeks. Write the config-PDA exposure into the accepted-limits list with round 34's framing, and have the ceremony's preflight name the squat as permanent, not as a "conflict" a retry could clear.

## FINDING 2 — LOW. V41-1's "initialize still lands on the dusted address" rests on one un-bundled run; pin it

**Construct.** `audits/round41/VALIDATION.md` claims the ceremony's `initialize` lands on a dusted (System-owned, zero-data, lamports>0) config address, citing `.cache/r41b-web-e2e.log` (129/129). `web/test/e2e-localnet.mjs` is not in this bundle, and no test in this bundle lands an initialize on dust — `ceremony.test.mjs`'s V41-1 arms check the read and the build only.

**Why it matters.** The claim is the only thing standing between "dust is harmless" and "dust is Finding 1 at half the price". It is also in tension with the project's own round-34 measurement: if account creation refuses pre-funded targets for the loader, the same system-program semantics apply to Anchor `init`. Either the loader's refusal is loader-specific (and the e2e run is right), or the e2e run is not measuring a real initialize against real dust.

**Evidence that would settle it.** The landed initialize's signature, and the resulting config account's lamports (rent exactly, or rent plus the dust?) — one line in the validation record. If the account holds rent only, the dust went somewhere it should not have; if rent plus dust, the semantics are pinned and the extra lamports are a harmless permanent resident of the config PDA.

**Fix.** Record the signature and the post-initialize lamports; add a suite arm that initializes over a pre-funded config PDA and asserts the resulting balance, so the semantics are a gate rather than a log.

## R1. The crank (`bank/crank.mjs`)

I looked for each named failure class and did not find one:

- **A plan the program will refuse.** The reveal plan is FIFO from `revealed_total` and stops at the first not-ready record, matching `RevealOutOfOrder`; the send loop breaks on the first failure *and* on the first benign (a traffic reveal of the head makes the crank's own send `AlreadyRevealed`, benign, and it never sends the next one out of order). `planCostLamports` counts exactly the transactions `main()` sends (per-batch ceilings for preJoins, preExpires, postExpires and claims; one tx each for the singles; reveal rent per reveal). The distribution refusal is re-judged against a fresh config read, with `PoolShort` never a race. `splitDecimalsByte` refuses a null mint read instead of sending decimals 0.
- **A plan that pays or joins a dead vault.** `retireBurned` runs first every pass; preJoins, claims and preExpires each skip positively-burned assets; an unreadable ('other') asset is never joined or paid and is reported as a problem, which also suppresses the distribution (`JoinWaitingFirst` would otherwise refuse it).
- **Value movement.** The selftest's gate (no SPL/System transfer, only MINT instructions, free-path distribute) holds against the source.
- **Arithmetic.** I re-derived the owed math for the three boost cases (pre-expiry, post-expiry, live boost) and the preJoined case against MINT's `credit`/`reweigh`/`join`/`owed_of`; the planner's formulas are exact, including the subtle fact that expiring a boost after a distribution does not change what the vault is owed (the carry absorbs it), which the planner's `else` branch computes correctly by accident of algebra — and the L4 arms pin the numbers.
- **Budgeting.** `budgetPlan` never spends the key below the floor through reveals and still sends the captures when under it; the SPEC sentence now matches the code (R0, C03).

One observation, not a defect: a malformed Core-owned account at a vault asset (data[0]==1 but shorter than a real AssetV1) classifies as 'live' and would be refused by the program's `require_live_vault`, costing one fee and one failure page. Not reachable by a stranger (Core owns the account).

## R5. The swap and the revenue cycle

- **(a) Oracle math.** The exponent handling is correct for both signs (`e ≥ 0` scales the numerator, `e < 0` the denominator); both divisions floor, so `minSats` is a true floor; the BigInt→Number conversions in `satsPerLamport` are only used for the deviation filter, where double precision (~15–16 digits) is orders of magnitude tighter than a 100 bps gate. The 150 bps floor covers Orca's 16 bps fee plus ~1–2 bps of impact for a 10 SOL swap in a $10M pool, and ~30 bps on the thinner Meteora pool — comfortably, and the fork's SW.8/SW.9 record the executed prices against the oracle.
- **(b) Pyth verification.** The offsets (level at 40, feed 41, price 73, conf 81, expo 89, publishTime 93) are the ones the selftest fixtures and the fork's publish-time rewrite both use; the account is a derived PDA of the push-oracle program (shard 0 + feed id) owned by the receiver, fully verified, feed-id-matched. A stranger cannot write it (only the push program can sign for its PDA); an old `publishTime` is bounded by the 180 s age; a chosen price is bounded by the confidence check, the 100 bps pool-agreement gate and the 150 bps on-chain floor. The clock is the Clock sysvar — trustworthy against anything short of a validator-majority attack, which is outside this threat model.
- **(c) Deviation filter.** `orcaSpot` is (sqrtPrice/2^64)² in raw cbBTC-per-lamport, which is `satsPerLamport/10` — the same quantity the oracle computes; `meteoraSpot` is the inverse bin power, reversed from Orca as the mints are. The fork measured both real pools inside the bound (SW.0). Floating point at the boundary is inclusive at exactly 100 bps, which is immaterial (the filter is a sanity gate; the floor is separate and on-chain).
- **(d) Minimum out.** Both venues carry `minSats` at data offset 16, in cbBTC base units — the same sats the oracle floor is denominated in. The deviation filter and the floor anchor to the same oracle read (the fresh one at swap-build time), and the return is held to exactly `guaranteed` after the swap.
- **(e) The judge.** It checks the exact seven-instruction sequence, the CU limit with no priority fee (any second budget instruction breaks the length or slot checks), both ATA creations for the operator's own wallet and mint, the wrap's exact amount and destination, SyncNative, the swap's amount/threshold/direction/price-limit/output account/signer slot, and the single close back to the operator. What it does not pin: the pool-side accounts (vaults, tick arrays, oracles — validated by the venue programs themselves) and Meteora's `host_fee_in` slot. A host-fee account cannot extract value: if the fee comes out of the output, the floor refuses the swap; if out of the LP fee, our output is unaffected. The same-tree tamper residue is stated and unchanged.
- **(f) Derivation.** Orca's three tick arrays (current, then the two below for a_to_b) with the string-seeded PDA; Meteora's bin arrays (active, then the two above for Y→X) with the i64-le seed; the bitmap passed or the program id as None; the event authority and two token programs. All fork-proven against the real programs. A boundary tick starts its own array (armed); an uninitialised array makes the venue program refuse, which is the fallback path.
- **(g) wSOL.** Wrap exactly `lamportsIn`, sync, exact-input swap, close. A pre-existing balance is preserved (SyncNative syncs the whole account, the swap sells exactly the input, the close returns everything — R33.G4 measured it); a frozen or delegated wSOL account is a box-compromise class (the native mint has no freeze authority and the key is ours). Nothing is left wrapped on any path: a failed swap reverts the whole transaction. The one standing grief here is the operator-ATA squat (Finding 1).
- **(h) Failure and retry.** Orca first; Meteora only on a refusal (preflight, landed-with-error, or expired-unlanded — `classifySendFailure` asks the chain). An unconfirmed send is 'unknown', throws, and leaves the cycle open; the next run is refused at the open-cycle guard; the candidate signature is preserved as evidence. A confirmation timeout on a swap that landed is recorded as landed. No path double-swaps, and `cycleIsOpen` cannot read a failed cycle as closed (a return of zero is open; a return without a split is open). One path stays manual by design: a return whose confirmation timed out leaves the cycle open with `returnedSats` unset and pages `unreconciled_swap` until a hand records it — the runbook covers it, and it is the loud direction.
- **(i) Extraction within tolerance.** A sandwich bounded by the 100 bps read-time gate can net at most ~100 bps of a cycle minus the attacker's own round-trip cost in a $10M pool — call it ≤ ~$6–8 per 10 SOL cycle, ≤ ~$1.5–2k a month at the maximum ~40 SOL/day throughput, and materially less in practice. A stale-but-accepted oracle mostly causes refusals rather than bad fills (a pool within 100 bps of the stale oracle executes at the pool's real price; a pool beyond it is skipped). A manipulated Meteora pool only matters when Orca refuses, and is then bounded by the same 150 bps floor. The cycle size makes this small; the number above is worth adding to limit 10's text, which currently states the qualitative bound only.
- **Operator key worst case:** unchanged from limit 10 — cap × cooldown windows until the guardian stops the sweep, bounded by the revenue PDA's balance above the reserve; the ledger is held to the chain wherever the alarm can see it, and a failed cycle cannot read as closed.

## GIVEN THAT NO HUMAN FIRM WILL EVER SEE THIS AND THE PROGRAMS WILL BE IMMUTABLE, what should this project do before launch that AI audit structurally cannot?

1. **Have a person run the gates and read the output, not the exit code.** Every number in this certification was produced by the project's own runs and read back to it. One person who did not write the code should execute `CBOB_SHIP_GATE=1 scripts/run-tests.sh`, `sim/run-sim.sh`, `sim/swap-fork.sh`, `web/test/run-all.mjs` and the launch checker on a fresh terminal and compare the printed counts to the floors by eye. An AI round can be fed a stale log; a person watching the scroll cannot.
2. **Close the squatting window with a human clock (Finding 1).** Create the pool, revenue and operator ATAs now; run road step 10 the day the charter date is set. The window's length is a human decision, and no assertion can shorten it.
3. **Run the dress rehearsal (item 22) with a human operating the runbook.** The named items — live crank, live alarm in rehearsal mode, one full revenue cycle through each pool with Orca forced to refuse once, one forced post-swap failure walked through hand recovery, the DAS read — and specifically: have someone who did not write `bank/revenue.mjs` perform the hand recovery from limit 10's runbook alone, and fix the runbook where they get stuck. That is the only test of whether the recovery is executable by the person who will one day need it at 3 a.m.
4. **Do a restore drill.** The whole detection story — ledger, state, cursors, keys — dies with this box. Restore the nightly backup onto a fresh machine and confirm the keys, ledgers and alarm state come back. An AI audit can read that a file is mode 600; it cannot verify that the backup restores.
5. **Make the custody decisions only a human can make, and physically verify them:** move the $CBOB release wallet off the box (limit 56's own stated closure), decide the PoB hot key's fate through the charter window (limit 46), and hand-verify the deploy-authority and crank/operator key files and their backup inclusion.
6. **Reproduce the build on a second machine.** `build-mainnet.sh`'s own closing claim is that reproducibility is the bridge for the one thing it cannot prove (that the compiled INIT_AUTHORITY is Gary's key). One independent rebuild of `dist/` from the release commit, hashes compared, closes that bridge for real.
7. **Pay someone to attack the rehearsal.** Dust the rehearsal's config addresses, pad transactions for log truncation, flood the scan, race the sweep — against throwaway ids, with real fees. Every one of those vectors is stated in SPEC; none has ever been executed *by an adversary who isn't also the author*.
8. **Run an alert drill to a real phone.** The alarm's entire value is a human reading a page. One deliberate page (pull the guardian's sweep stop in rehearsal) delivered, read and acted on end-to-end.
9. **Decide the X promise.** The holder guide says every opening is announced. Either Gary authorizes the sender and the website surface before item 50 ships, or the sentence comes out. That is a product decision no audit can make.

**Bottom line.** Round 41's repairs all hold under independent reading, and I found no defect in the crank, the alarm, the layouts, the accrual mirror, the swap, the revenue cycle or the web tests that survives measurement-by-reading. What survives is one HIGH operational hole the documents do not carry — the squatting window, open today, permanent, priced in dust — and one LOW verification ask that would pin the semantics its repair depends on. Both are fixable before anything mints, and neither is fixable after.

Everything is committed and pushed to the private repo:

Nothing has minted and nothing holds real value. That has not changed and

WHAT I RAN

8 independent auditors this round. First four were families never used on this
code: deepseek-v4-pro, qwen3.8-max, glm-5.3, minimax-m3. Then, on your
instruction, the top model of three families: gpt-5.6-terra-pro,
grok-4.20-multi-agent, kimi-k3, plus claude-opus-5 for comparison.

I pointed them at the three surfaces that had the least scrutiny: the
cross-program economics, the "seal config is canonical" pattern, and the
Attributes provenance stamp.


THE HEADLINE

11 real findings fixed, 5 rejected. Your warning about the auditors held: about
a third of the confident claims were wrong again, and three of the rejections I
settled by measuring on a validator rather than by arguing.

The two that matter most:

1. There was NO lifetime supply cap. The field that was supposed to enforce it,
   minted_total, was written on every mint and read by nothing. Its own comment
   in the code named the exact attack it was meant to prevent. All 8 auditors
   found it. The ~2,100 vault supply was an off-chain promise with zero on-chain
   enforcement, and per-round caps reset on every new round. Fixed: max_supply
   now gates every mint and can only ratchet down, never up.

2. The BURN program's reserve list was not actually canonical (found by glm-5.3
   alone, then upheld by four others). It kept its own copy of the reserve mints
   and only cross-checked the collection and the bank. So adding a mint to the
   seal program and forgetting to add it to burn, two separate admin actions,
   left that mint never derived, never required and never checked. A vault
   holding it would burn cleanly and the tokens would be destroyed, with your
   signature and the owner's on it. The header comment claimed the cross-check
   prevented exactly that. It did not. Burn now reads the list out of the seal
   config, so there is no second copy that can disagree.

Also fixed: charter provenance was never being written at all, and the first
honest redemption would have wiped the Attributes list anyway. That is the
record that survives after the Proof of Burn token and the Notice are both
destroyed, so a charter member redeeming once would have lost the only proof
they were charter. No attacker needed. The happy path did it. Provenance is now
stamped at mint and preserved through every redemption, and there is a test that
mints a charter vault, redeems it, and asserts the serial is still there.


THE ONE THAT WOULD HAVE COST YOU MONEY IF I HAD TRUSTED IT

grok-4.20 recommended dropping a check in the burn program, saying Core sweeps
the leftover SOL to the payer. gpt-5.6 and kimi both said that was mechanically
false. I tested it: after a real burn, the vault's PDA still held all 5,000,000
lamports and the payer got back only the asset's own rent. Core's burn
instruction never even receives that account, so it cannot move it. Taking
grok's advice would have permanently destroyed customer SOL on every burn.

This is why nothing gets applied unread.


THE ONE ONLY OPUS FOUND

If a Token-2022 mint were ever added to the reserve list, every vault holding it
would be sealed forever, with no recovery for you or the holder. The reason is
small and nasty: a Token-2022 associated token account is 170 bytes, not 165,
and both reserve checks demand exactly 165 (deliberately, because a Token-2022
confidential balance reads as zero). That makes the close instruction fail
permanently, and opening a vault is illegal without a close.

It does not fire today. I checked all three reserve mints on mainnet and cbBTC,
$GARY and $GIRAFFISH are all classic SPL. But the reserve list is additive by
design, so this was one ordinary config change away from arming. Reserve mints
are now checked when they enter the config, which turns a customer losing their
Bitcoin into an admin seeing an error message.


WHAT I BUILT

The governance program. One vault, one vote, exactly as you decided. Each vault
votes as itself: the voter presents the asset, the program reads the owner out
of the asset's bytes and requires that owner to have signed. No indexer, no
snapshot, standing proved against live chain state at the moment of the vote.

The vote is bound to the ASSET, not the wallet. Vote, transfer the vault to
someone else, and the new owner cannot vote again with it. There is a test that
does exactly that and it rejects, and it rejects specifically because the vote
record already exists rather than for some incidental reason. A ratified vote
can do exactly one thing: set a round on the mint program. Even a hostile
majority cannot mint past the supply cap.

The Bank alarm pipeline. This is the part you flagged as the real remaining risk
and you were right. Once the Bank's veto became load-bearing, a real share of
the guarantee moved off-chain, and nothing was watching it. It now alerts by
email on: any delegate approval against a vault (a delegate survives the reseal
and can drain a vault that still looks sealed and full), any token account
created for a vault, any burn by any route, any vault that stops reading as
activated, and any drift on the treasury, governance, admin, supply cap, reserve
list or voting period.

Every detector is proven to fire AND to stay quiet, 22 assertions. A read that
fails is treated as an alarm, never as a pass, and the report says what it
actually looked at rather than "all clear".


TESTS

64 assertions against a local validator running the real Metaplex Core cloned
from mainnet, with real SPL tokens. Was 19 when I started.

Worth telling you: the old 19/19 could not be reproduced. The runner had four
separate faults, each of which independently produced a green-looking run that
proved nothing. The worst was that the command to kill the old validator matched
nothing at all (Linux truncates process names at 15 characters), so the old
validator survived, the new one died on a port clash, and the deploys landed on
the surviving one, which had no Core on it. A green run against the wrong
validator is worse than a red one. The runner now refuses to deploy unless Core
on the validator is byte for byte identical to the mainnet copy, and that check
is proven to fire in both directions.

Three tests in this round also "passed" for the wrong reason at first, rejecting
on a malformed account list before the code being tested ever ran. Every
negative test now asserts why it rejected, not just that it did.


WHAT I NEED FROM YOU BEFORE THIS GOES NEAR MAINNET

1. The Squads multisig address. Four programs have a placeholder constant that
   must be the multisig before the mainnet build, and the upgrade authorities
   have to move there too. Until they do, "no key can unseal a vault" is false
   by one indirection.

2. Confirm one trade-off I made. Minting now requires the vault's owner to sign.
   That makes it impossible to mint vaults FOR members who are not present, so
   bulk charter issuance to a list of addresses will not work. I chose it because
   a vault minted to an address nobody can sign for is unredeemable forever and
   there is no way to detect that on-chain. If you need bulk issuance, tell me
   and I will find a different answer rather than weaken the check.

3. A decision on the 85/15 split. It is not enforced on-chain at all. 100% of
   the mint price goes to one treasury account and the split is a promise. Three
   auditors read the code comment as though the program enforced it. Either we
   enforce it on-chain or we stop implying it in public copy.

4. The voting period. It is a config value with a 60 second floor, which only
   exists so the execute path can actually be tested. The deployed value must be
   days. The alarm watches for this being left short.

5. A human audit firm on a frozen version. Five AI rounds have each found
   something real, including this one. They sharpen it. They do not sign it off.


One honest note on where this is heading. The seal program itself has now
survived five rounds and the last three found nothing in it. The findings have
moved to economics, configuration and the parts that did not exist yet, which is
what you predicted last time. The remaining risk is concentrated in the off-chain
Bank duties, which now at least have a watcher, and in the operational setup
above.
